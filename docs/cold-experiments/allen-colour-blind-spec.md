## 2. What the paper actually is

It **is** an empirical paper — but a *qualitative* one, not a questionnaire study. It reports
semi-structured interviews with colour-blind participants, and is framed as a methodological case
study for "the nascent field of experimental philosophy of experience" (p. 1).

> "What is the experience of someone who is 'colour-blind' like? This paper presents the results of a
> study that uses qualitative research methods to better understand the lived experience of colour
> blindness. Participants were asked to describe their experiences of a variety of coloured stimuli,
> both with and without EnChroma glasses — glasses which, the manufacturers claim, enhance the
> experience of people with common forms of colour blindness." (abstract, p. 1)

The paper's stated three aims (pp. 4–6): (i) adjudicate between competing accounts of the
phenomenology of colour blindness; (ii) assess EnChroma glasses "from a phenomenological
perspective" (p. 5); (iii) assess "the prospects of using qualitative research methods in
experimental philosophy (x-phi)" (p. 5).

### 2.1 The four competing accounts (the paper's own taxonomy)

These are the four options the classroom task's primary item is built from. Verbatim:

1. **Standard view** — "The standard view of colour blindness, and the view that the colloquial name
   'colour blindness' suggests, is that someone who is (e.g.,) red/green colour-blind is unable to see
   reds and greens — they are blind to these colours." (p. 1). Quoting Palmer's textbook, such
   perceivers "fail to experience red and green hues, both of which are presumably seen as grays. As a
   result, the world appears to them in various shades of blues, yellows, and grays, as though the
   red/green dimension of the color solid has been eliminated (Palmer, 1999, p. 104)." (p. 2)

2. **Alien colour view** — "the *alien colour view* denies that people who are colour-blind see even
   blue, yellow, grey, white or black. On this view, the colours that colour-blind perceivers see
   cannot be located in trichromatic human colour space at all." (p. 3)

3. **Revised reduction / revised alien view** (Byrne & Hilbert 2010) — "colour-blind and normal
   trichromatic perceivers have the same experiences of achromatic colours (white, black and grey) and
   unique yellow and unique blue… However, like the alien colour view, they argue that people who are
   colour-blind perceive colours that normal trichromatic perceivers never perceive. This is because
   they perceive binary hues as being simply yellowish or bluish (to different degrees)" (p. 3), quoting:
   > "[N]ormal trichromats never see this hue [yellowish or bluish] without seeing more determinate hues
   > like orange and yellow. On the Revised Reduction View, a red-green dichromat sees yellowishness
   > unaccompanied. (Byrne & Hilbert, 2010, p. 280)." (p. 3)

4. **Common colour view** — "colour-blind perceivers are genuinely able to perceive the colours that
   they are supposedly 'blind to,' at least in favourable conditions." (p. 3). Weaker/stronger forms:
   reds and greens "might appear to be elementary hues… but appear to CVD perceivers to be darker or
   lighter and as having a lower degree of 'chromaticness'" (p. 4).

### 2.2 The context-dependence claim (basis of the classroom between-subjects manipulation)

> "The ability of colour-blind perceivers to perceive these colours depends in part on the type of
> stimulus and the way it is presented: Perception of reds and greens is improved if the stimulus is a
> material object rather than a spectral light, if the object is well-illuminated, if the stimulus
> (object or light) occupies a sufficiently large area in the visual field, or is presented for a
> sufficient length of time" (p. 4)

> "Smith and Pokorny (1977) found that dichromats tend to perform more like some form of trichromat
> when the stimulus occupies more than 8° of the visual field." (p. 4)

And from the results, participant #6 on the same red seen small vs. large (p. 16):

> "The sample is too small to tell … The thing about the door is that I've got a very large panel of
> red to observe there, a small chip like that is hard to make a judgement about … I would say it looks
> darker actually but I don't know that it looks, does it look browner? I don't know. Sorry there's an
> ambiguity in this. (#6)." (p. 16)

> "In general, there was evidence from a number of participants that context makes an important
> difference to how well colours can be perceived: Participants who expressed difficulties, or made
> mistakes, in one context, were often able to easily and correctly identify the very same colour in a
> different context." (p. 16)

## 3. The paper's actual paradigm (verbatim capture)

### 3.1 Participants and procedure (p. 6)

- "17 colour-blind participants were interviewed (16 males, 1 female, average age 38.5)." (p. 6)
- Sample size justified by "saturation," "the point at which additional participants repeated
  responses or comments made by earlier participants and no new themes emerged in the analysis." (p. 6)
- "All participants were staff or students at the University of York." (p. 6)
- "Each session lasted for approximately 1 hr." (p. 6)
- Screened with **Ishihara plates**. "The 16 male participants all displayed some form of red/green
  CVD; the female participant was a near achromat (she is referred to as #13*…)." (p. 6)
- Footnote 11: "The test provisionally suggested that the sample contained eight protans and three
  deutans; six participants were unclassified." (p. 6)
- Design: within-subject **phenomenal contrast** — tasks done first without, then with, EnChroma
  glasses after "10 min to acclimatise" (p. 7); `ELEMENTARIES` was **not** repeated with glasses (p. 7).
- Glasses: "Receptor Fitovers with Cx3 Sun lenses" (p. 8).
- Comparison group for the naming counts: "20 normal trichromatic perceivers" (p. 11).
- Sessions audio-recorded, transcribed, thematically analysed in **NVivo12** (p. 9).
- Ethics: Arts and Humanities Ethics Committee, University of York (p. 9).

### 3.2 The four tasks (verbatim, pp. 7–8)

**ELEMENTARIES** (p. 7): "Participants were presented with a representation of NCS colour space showing
the NCS elementary colours (the elementary hues plus black and white). For each colour, participants
were given 30 s to name as many things, either in the room or in the world, that looked to be that
colour. Once they had done this for all six colours, they were then asked to name them. Colours were
presented in the following order: green, blue, white, black, yellow and red. The representation was
printed on B2 paper (50 × 70 cm) and each colour was 6 cm diameter."

**HUECIRCLE** (p. 7): "Participants were asked to describe what they saw as they scanned clockwise
around the NCS colour circle with the NCS notation obscured (Figure 1). They were also asked to
identify the colours at the cardinal and ordinal compass points in a set order: yellow, blue-green,
yellow-green, red, blue, orange, green and purple. The representation was on B2 paper and individual
patches were 3.8 × 2 cm."

**REDTRIANGLE** (p. 8): "Participants were asked to describe their experience of an NCS colour triangle
(also on B2 paper), the right-hand apex of which was Y90R, the red sample adjacent to due east on the
hue circle (Figure 3). First, they traced a horizontal line from the right-hand apex, across a line
where the samples differ in chromaticness. Second, they scanned around the perimeter of the triangle…"

**SORTING** (p. 8): "Participants were asked to sort 12 coloured papers into 'groups that seem natural'
to them; they could sort them into as many piles as they wanted… Four of the papers were colours
adjacent to the cardinal compass points of the NCS colour circle (Y90R, R90B, G10Y Y10R). Four papers
were lighter versions of these colours and four papers were darker versions of these colours. The
papers were 2.3 × 2.3 cm presented on a white background."

### 3.4 Conclusions (verbatim)

> "The results of this study militate against the standard view of colour blindness, according to which
> someone who is red/green colour-blind is completely unable to see reds and greens, but sees only
> yellows, blues, blacks, whites, and shades of grey" (p. 19)

> "The current results also militate against both the alien colour and revised reduction/revised alien
> views." (p. 20)

> "The current results instead support the common colour view, complementing existing evidence that
> colour-blind perceivers are able to perceive reds and green, at least when the conditions are
> favourable." (p. 21)

> "it was differences in some participants' experiences of pinks and purples that were the most
> striking." (p. 23)

## 4. Why the paper's paradigm cannot be run as-is in class

The paradigm requires (a) **colour-blind participants**, screened with Ishihara plates; (b) **physical
NCS-licensed B2 print stimuli** under controlled daylight; (c) **EnChroma Cx3 glasses**; (d) hour-long
one-to-one semi-structured interviews transcribed and coded in NVivo. A browser-based classroom
experiment run on a mixed cohort of (mostly) trichromats on uncalibrated laptops satisfies none of
these.

**Therefore the built experiment is an ORIGINAL design *inspired by* the paper, not a replication.**
It is a **third-person** study: it measures what *sighted, mostly-trichromatic students* believe and
predict about colour-blind experience, and then confronts those predictions with Allen et al.'s
first-person findings. This inversion is what makes it classroom-runnable, and it targets a claim the
paper **asserts but does not itself test** — that the standard view is what "the colloquial name
'colour blindness' suggests" (p. 1) and enjoys "widespread acceptance" (p. 3). Those are empirical
claims about lay belief. The classroom experiment tests them.

**⚠ This design needs Nat's sign-off. It is not in the paper.**

## 5. The built design

Total time ≈ 3–4 minutes. Order is fixed and matters: Task 1 must come first so the primary DV is
uncontaminated by having seen simulated swatches.

### Task 1 — primary DV, BETWEEN-subjects

Participant is randomly assigned (uniform, client-side at mount) to one of two viewing-context
conditions, operationalizing the favourability claim at p. 4:

- **`unfavourable`** — a small (2 cm) chip of strong red on a white card, glanced at briefly under
  dim indoor light, across the room.
- **`favourable`** — a large, evenly painted strong-red door, filling much of the visual field, in
  bright daylight, looked at for as long as they like.

Single forced choice, four options, presented in a **randomized order per participant** (order is
recorded), each option being a plain-English rendering of one of the paper's four views:

| Key | Option gloss (as shown) | Maps to |
|---|---|---|
| `standard` | "A shade of grey. The red/green dimension is simply missing from their experience." | Standard view (Palmer, p. 2) |
| `alien` | "A colour you have never seen and cannot imagine — one with no place anywhere in your colour space." | Alien colour view (p. 3) |
| `revised` | "Yellowish, or bluish — but not any more specific shade than that. Just yellowishness, on its own." | Revised reduction/revised alien (Byrne & Hilbert, p. 3) |
| `common` | "Red — genuinely red, though perhaps duller, darker, or browner than it looks to you." | Common colour view (p. 3–4) |

Plus a 1–5 confidence slider.

**Predictions.** (a) `standard` will be modal overall, vindicating the paper's untested claim about
what the colloquial name suggests. (b) The `favourable`/`unfavourable` manipulation will move
responses **little or not at all** — folk third-person judgements are expected to be
context-invariant, whereas Allen et al.'s first-person data are strongly context-dependent (pp. 4, 16).
That gap is the teaching point.

### Task 2 — WITHIN-subjects hands-on: naming simulated swatches

Six large sRGB swatches, each rendered through a **deuteranopia** simulation (see §6). Prompt: if
this is how the patch looks to a red/green colour-blind viewer, what would you call it? Eight fixed
naming options: red, orange, yellow, green, blue, purple, brown, grey.

| # | Original hue (NCS-style label) | sRGB in | Deuteranope sRGB out | Expected naming |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | elementary red (≈Y90R) | `#C8102E` | `#737320` | brown / yellow / grey — **not** red |
| 2 | elementary green (≈G) | `#00875A` | `#73735C` | grey / brown — **not** green |
| 3 | elementary yellow (≈Y) | `#F2C500` | `#D3D300` | yellow (preserved) |
| 4 | elementary blue (≈B) | `#0B6FA4` | `#5F5FA5` | blue (preserved) |
| 5 | purple (≈R50B) | `#7B3F98` | `#565697` | **blue** — the paper's key confusion |
| 6 | orange (≈Y50R) | `#E2711D` | `#9D9D00` | yellow — **not** orange |

Presentation order is fixed and interleaved (red, yellow, purple, green, blue, orange) so the
collapsing and preserved patches are not visibly grouped.

DV: count of swatches named with the **original** hue's name (0–6), split into

- `rgScore` (0–4) over the four patches whose hue **collapses** under the transform — red, green,
  purple, orange;
- `ybScore` (0–2) over the two the transform broadly **preserves** — yellow, blue.

All three scores are recomputed server-side in the POST route from the stored `namings` array
rather than trusted from the client, so the aggregate can never disagree with the raw answers.

Item 5 is deliberately included because the simulation independently reproduces the paper's most
robust confusion — "Thirteen participants either reported difficulties with, or made mistakes
involving, purple" (p. 14), with samples "often being described as blue" (p. 14).

### Task 3 — WITHIN-subjects: the new-colours prediction

"Suppose a red/green colour-blind person puts on glasses that enhance their colour vision. Which
colours would strike them as most new or most changed?" Five options: reds / greens / pinks and
purples / blues / yellows.

**Actual answer (p. 18–19):** "no one claimed to have seen reds or greens for the first time; all the
examples of possible new colours were pinks and purples" — and 12 of 17 (71%) reported no new colours
at all (p. 17). Expected student answer: reds and/or greens. This is the sharpest single reveal in the
debrief.

### Debrief

Reports the paper's real numbers (§3.3), names the four views and which one the paper endorses,
carries the verified DOI and an open-access link, and states the simulation caveats (§6.3).

## 6. Colour-vision-deficiency simulation method

### 6.1 Algorithm

Swatch colours are simulated using the standard **Viénot, Brettel & Mollon (1999)** single-plane
LMS projection for dichromats — the simplified form of the **Brettel, Viénot & Mollon (1997)**
algorithm, valid for protanopia and deuteranopia (the two-half-plane construction of the 1997 paper is
only needed for tritanopia).

- Viénot, F., Brettel, H., & Mollon, J. D. (1999). "Digital video colourmaps for checking the
  legibility of displays by dichromats." *Color Research & Application* **24**(4): 243–252.
  DOI `10.1002/(SICI)1520-6378(199908)24:4<243::AID-COL5>3.0.CO;2-3` (verified via Crossref).
- Brettel, H., Viénot, F., & Mollon, J. D. (1997). "Computerized simulation of color appearance for
  dichromats." *Journal of the Optical Society of America A* **14**(10): 2647–2655.
  DOI `10.1364/JOSAA.14.002647` (verified via Crossref).

Pipeline: sRGB → linearise (standard sRGB EOTF, 0.04045 / 12.92 / 2.4 breakpoint) → LMS via the
Viénot RGB→LMS matrix → collapse the missing cone channel → LMS→RGB via the inverse matrix →
re-apply the sRGB transfer function → clamp to [0, 255].

Deuteranope collapse: `M' = 0.494207·L + 1.24827·S` (L, S unchanged).
Protanope collapse (implemented, unused in the shipped stimuli): `L' = 2.02344·M − 2.52581·S`.

### 6.2 Precomputation

Because the six stimuli are flat swatches, the transform is applied **once at build time in the
source** and the resulting hex values are checked into the stimulus table (§5, Task 2), with the
transform itself also shipped as a documented helper so the numbers are auditable and reproducible.
No per-pixel image processing runs in the browser.

### 6.3 Limitations to state in the debrief

1. **Uncalibrated monitors.** Students view on unprofiled laptop and phone screens in arbitrary
   ambient light. The sRGB values assume a calibrated sRGB display; actual rendered chromaticities
   will vary substantially across the room. Absolute hue names are therefore soft; the
   red/green-vs-yellow/blue *contrast* is robust, the exact naming distribution is not.
2. **Dichromat, not anomalous trichromat.** The transform models a *dichromat* (deuteranope). Allen
   et al. note that "based on the relative prevalence of these conditions we would expect a
   substantial proportion of the participants to be anomalous trichromats" (p. 19), whose loss is far
   milder. The simulation therefore overstates the deficit for the most common form of CVD.
3. **The deepest caveat — the simulation presupposes the view the paper rejects.** A CVD simulation
   renders the dichromat's reduced gamut *in trichromatic colours on a trichromatic display*. It can
   only show what a dichromat cannot **discriminate**; it cannot show what their experience is
   **like**. Its output — reds and greens rendered as browns, olives and greys — is a picture of
   something close to the standard view, which is exactly the view Allen et al. conclude the evidence
   "militate[s] against" (p. 19). Students should be told the demo is an argument-prop, not evidence.
4. **Colour-blind students in the room.** Some participants will themselves be CVD. The task wording
   must not assume the participant is a trichromat, and the instructor should expect (and can use)
   divergent responses from CVD students.

## 7. Audit queue — items needing Nat's sign-off

1. **The whole design is original.** It is inspired by, and not a replication of, Allen et al. (2021).
   Its target — lay third-person beliefs about CVD experience — is a claim the paper asserts (pp. 1, 3)
   but never tests. Nat should decide whether that inversion is worth running.
2. **The four option glosses in Task 1** are my plain-English compressions of the paper's four views.
   They are the load-bearing stimulus text. Each is traceable to the quotes in §2.1, but the wording is
   mine and will propagate verbatim to every student. **Hand-audit required.**
3. **All intro and debrief prose** glossing the paper is mine. Numbers are page-cited above and should
   be spot-checked against the table in §3.3.
4. **The `favourable` / `unfavourable` vignettes** operationalize p. 4's list of favourability factors
   (material object, illumination, angular size, exposure time). I bundled several factors into each
   vignette rather than varying one; this maximizes effect size but confounds the factors. Confirm
   that trade-off is acceptable for a classroom demo.
5. **Swatch sRGB inputs** are my approximations to NCS elementary and binary hues, not licensed NCS
   values. NCS notations are given as "≈" labels only. If exactness matters, they need replacing —
   and note the paper's own NCS figures are reproduced "by permission of NCS Colour AB" (pp. 6, 7, 8),
   so NCS artwork must **not** be copied into the site.
6. **Predicted results** in §5 are my expectations, not findings. Nothing in the shipped debrief
   asserts what the class will do.

## 8. Data record

One JSON blob per participant at `experiments/allen-colour-blind/<id>.json`:

One JSON blob per participant, written with `access: "private"`, `addRandomSuffix: false`,
`allowOverwrite: false` — the repo's standard cold-experiment pattern.

```ts
{
  session: string,             // sanitized; path segment
  tag?: string,                // opaque ?tag=; key ABSENT (never null) when unset
  submittedAt: string,         // client ISO timestamp
  durationMs: number,
  study: 1,                    // parity with the other experiments
  condition: "favourable" | "unfavourable",
  viewChoice: "standard" | "alien" | "revised" | "common",
  viewOrder: string,           // pipe-joined permutation, e.g. "common|standard|revised|alien"
  confidence: number,          // 1–5
  viewRtMs: number,
  choseStandard, choseAlien, choseRevised, choseCommon: boolean,   // one-hot, see below
  namings: string[],           // 6 names, parallel to [red, yellow, purple, green, blue, orange]
  namingScore: number,         // 0–6, server-recomputed
  rgScore: number,             // 0–4 (red, purple, green, orange)
  ybScore: number,             // 0–2 (yellow, blue)
  newColours: "reds"|"greens"|"pinks-purples"|"blues"|"yellows",
  choseNewPinksPurples: boolean,
  selfReportCVD: "yes" | "no" | "unsure" | null
}
```

The four `chose*` booleans are redundant with `viewChoice`. They exist so the **generic**
`ASYMMETRY_SPECS` summarizer in `class-summary/route.ts` can report the whole 4-way distribution as
four proportions per condition cell, with no bespoke aggregation code on either side.

`selfReportCVD` is asked **at the end** (so it cannot prime Task 1) and lets the instructor split
trichromat from CVD respondents on the dashboard. The dashboard also carries an
"exclude colour-blind" toggle.
